How to Find US Clients as a Nigerian Freelancer: A Practical Guide to Cold Email & Lead Generation

August 10, 2026 · 9 min read · By Freelance LeadsHub

If you're a Nigerian freelancer, you already know the problem: Upwork and Fiverr are flooded. You're bidding against 50 other freelancers for a $500 project, and half of them will undercut your rate just to win the gig. Your hourly earnings collapse to ₦2,000–₦3,000 ($1.50–$2.50 USD), and after Payoneer or Wise fees, you're left with even less.

But here's the reality: US clients will pay 2–4x more than you can earn on those platforms. A US business owner looking for a copywriter, designer, or virtual assistant isn't scrolling Fiverr. They're either hiring through referrals or responding to cold outreach from professionals who know how to pitch.

This guide shows you exactly how to find US clients as a Nigerian freelancer—using cold email, targeted lead lists, and strategies that actually work. By the end, you'll have a system to land US clients directly, negotiate fair rates (USD 25–75/hour, not $5), and build a client base that doesn't depend on auction platforms.

Why Cold Email Works for Nigerian Freelancers

Cold email isn't new. But for Nigerian freelancers, it's a game-changer because:

  • You bypass Upwork's bidding wars. You're reaching the business owner directly, not competing in a marketplace.
  • You control the narrative. You pitch your specific value—not your portfolio rank or ratings.
  • US businesses expect to pay professional rates. A small business in Texas paying $5,000/month for virtual assistant work isn't price-sensitive about $25/hour vs. $35/hour. They want reliability and results.
  • You can send hundreds of emails cheaply. A well-researched email costs you almost nothing to send, but one reply can turn into a $5,000–$15,000/month client.

To put it in context: if you land just 3–4 US clients paying $40/hour for 10 hours/week each, you're earning ₦480,000–₦640,000/month in base income (at ₦800 exchange rate). That's sustainable. That's a business. That's not "gig work"—that's a freelance career.

Step 1: Choose Your Niche and Target (US Only)

Cold email fails when you're generic. "I'm a freelancer—I can do anything" doesn't work. You need to niche down and pick a specific US market.

Examples that work well for Nigerian freelancers:

  • Virtual Assistant/Admin Support — US small businesses (10–50 employees) need offshore admin help. They'll pay $20–30/hour for someone reliable.
  • Copywriting & Content Writing — US e-commerce stores, SaaS companies, and agencies always need copy. Rates: $40–100/hour or per-project $500–5,000.
  • Bookkeeping & Accounting — Accountants and bookkeepers in the US are swamped. They hire offshore support for data entry and reconciliation. Rates: $25–50/hour.
  • Customer Service/Support — Tech companies and e-commerce businesses need 24/7 support. Your timezone advantage (Nigeria is 5–8 hours ahead of US) is valuable.
  • Social Media Management — Agencies and small brands need someone to manage Instagram, Facebook, LinkedIn. Rates: $500–2,000/month retainer.
  • Graphic Design & Video Editing — Always in demand. Rates vary widely ($25–75/hour or $1,000–5,000 per project).

Pick ONE. Master it. Once you have 2–3 clients in that niche, you'll get referrals and specialisation works in your favour.

Step 3: Craft Your Cold Email (The Pitch That Works)

Your email is short. It's personal. It's about THEM, not you.

Here's a template that works:

  1. Subject line: Something specific and curiosity-driven. Not "Virtual Assistant Services" — try "[Business name], quick thought on your [specific problem]" or "Your team's time back?"
  2. Greeting: Use the person's actual name. "Hi Sarah" beats "Hi there" every time.
  3. Opening: Show you know their business. One sentence. "I noticed you run a Shopify store focused on [product]. I've worked with 3 similar brands on [specific task]."
  4. The ask: Propose ONE specific outcome. "I manage customer emails and order processing for US brands. Most clients save 15–20 hours/week." Not "I'm a VA and can do anything."
  5. Social proof: One line. "Worked with [client type] at [company]. Result: [metric]." Optional if you don't have it yet—skip it and focus on specificity instead.
  6. CTA: Make it easy to say yes. "Can I send you a 2-minute video showing how this works?" or "Are you open to a brief call next week?" Not "Let me know if you're interested."
  7. Sign-off: Your name, title, and one contact method (email + phone is fine).

Full example:

"Hi Michael,

I noticed you run ServiceTitan support for plumbing contractors. Most plumbing shops I work with spend 8+ hours/week on customer follow-ups and scheduling. I manage that backend for contractors across Texas and Florida.

If you're interested in getting 10 hours back per week, I can walk you through how this works in a 15-minute call.

Are you available Tuesday or Wednesday afternoon?

Best,
[Your name]
Virtual Operations Manager
+234 [phone] or email"

Key rules:

  • Keep it under 100 words. They'll scan it in 20 seconds.
  • No long paragraphs. Use line breaks.
  • No "I'm a Nigerian freelancer" — never mention location unless asked. Your work speaks.
  • No attachments. Just plain text or one link.
  • No desperation. You're offering value, not begging for work.

Step 5: Follow Up Strategically

Your first email has a 1–2% reply rate. That's normal. Don't panic. Follow up.

Follow-up sequence (over 2–3 weeks):

  • Email 1 (Day 0): Your initial pitch.
  • Email 2 (Day 5): "Hey [name], wanted to circle back on my last email. No pressure, but if you're managing [problem] right now, I might be able to help."
  • Email 3 (Day 12): Different angle. "Noticed you've [done something on your website/social]. Quick thought on how this ties into [your service]."
  • Email 4 (Day 20): Final one. "Final note—most of my clients are in [industry] and see [result]. If that's relevant, let's chat. If not, no worries."

Step 6: Convert Replies into Calls (and Calls into Clients)

You got a reply. "Hi, tell me more."

This is where most Nigerian freelancers fail. They send a long reply with their entire portfolio. Wrong move.

Reply strategy:

  1. Keep it brief. "Thanks for getting back to me. I'd rather show you quickly on a call—do you have 15 minutes Tuesday or Wednesday?"
  2. Get on a call ASAP. Email back-and-forth is slow. A 15-minute call closes more deals than 5 emails.
  3. On the call: Ask questions. Don't pitch. "What does your current workflow look like for [task]? Where's the bottleneck?" Let them talk. They'll tell you exactly what they need.
  4. Then propose a solution. "Based on what you said, I'd handle [X], [Y], [Z]. I typically charge $[rate] for this. Let's start with a 2-week trial so you can see if it's the right fit."
  5. Close with a contract. Use a simple agreement (Fiverr has templates, or use a basic Google Doc). Specify: scope, rate, hours per week, payment method (Wise, Payoneer, direct bank transfer).

Step 7: Handle Payments (USD to NGN Conversions)

Your first US client sends you USD 2,000. Now what?

You need a fast, cheap way to convert USD to NGN.

Best options for Nigerian freelancers:

  • Wise (formerly TransferWise): 1.5–2% fee, mid-market exchange rate. Fastest. If a US client is paying directly from their US bank, they can send to your Wise account, and you withdraw to a Nigerian bank. Takes 1–2 days.
  • Payoneer: Slightly higher fees (2–3%), but convenient if you have a Payoneer USD account. Many US clients know Payoneer.
  • Direct bank transfer (SWIFT): Higher fees (₦10,000–15,000), slower (3–5 days). Avoid unless the client insists.
  • Grey (remittance app): Good rates if the client uses a US fintech or app. Check current rates.

Be transparent with clients about this. "I accept USD payments via Wise or Payoneer. Once the payment arrives, I withdraw to my Nigerian bank the same day."

At current exchange rates (₦800–₦850 per USD), a client paying you USD 40/hour for 40 hours/month = USD 1,600 = ₦1.28M–₦1.36M/month. After fees (roughly 2%), you net ₦1.25M+/month from ONE client. That's life-changing income in Nigeria.

Common Mistakes Nigerian Freelancers Make

1. Being too polite or apologetic in emails. "Sorry to bother you" or "I hope this isn't a bad time" weakens your pitch. You're offering value. Act like it.

2. Overselling your Nigerian location. "I'm a hardworking Nigerian freelancer" sounds desperate. Your work speaks. If asked about location, say, "I'm based in Africa, 5–8 hours ahead of US time, which is actually perfect for US clients needing after-hours support."

3. Undercutting rates to win the first client. You land a US client by charging USD 15/hour instead of USD 30/hour. Bad move. You've set the baseline. They'll expect that rate forever, and you'll burn out.

4. Not following up. First email gets ignored. You assume they're not interested and move on. Reality: They're busy. Follow up 3–4 times over 3 weeks. Most conversions happen on the 2nd or 3rd email.

5. Not tracking metrics. You send 200 emails and don't know your open rate, click rate, or reply rate. How do you improve? Use Zaram or similar tools to track everything. If reply rate is 0.2%, your email copy needs work. If reply rate is 2% but conversion is 0%, your call strategy is weak.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Will US clients care that I'm in Nigeria?

A: Most won't ask. Your email doesn't mention location, and your work quality is what matters. If they ask, say you're timezone-advantaged (you work while they sleep, so tasks are done by morning). A few clients might prefer "US-based" workers, but most small businesses care about results and reliability, not geography. Just deliver quality work on time, and you'll be fine.

Q: How do I handle time zone differences with US clients?

A: Nigeria is 5–8 hours ahead of the US, which is actually an advantage. You work 9 AM–5 PM Nigerian time, and your client gets results by their 9 AM. If they need live calls, schedule 1–2 per week at a time that works (early morning for you, evening for them). Most async work (email, content, admin tasks) doesn't require real-time overlap.

Q: What if I don't have portfolio or past client work to show?

A: You don't need it for cold email. Focus on the outcome, not past work. "I've helped 3 e-commerce brands reduce customer service response time from 24 hours to 2 hours" works even if you haven't done that for a paying client yet—as long as you can deliver it. For your first client, offer a 2-week trial at a reduced rate or a money-back guarantee. This lowers their risk and gives you a portfolio piece.

Q: How many cold emails should I send to land one client?

A: Expect 1 client per 100–200 emails if your copy is good and you follow up properly. But this varies. If your niche has high demand (virtual assistants, customer service) and you target the right company size (10–100 employees), your conversion might be 1 per 50–100 emails. If you're too niche or email the wrong audience, it might be 1 per 500 emails. Track your metrics and adjust.
