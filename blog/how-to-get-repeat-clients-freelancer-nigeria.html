How to Get Repeat Clients as a Freelancer in Nigeria: A Practical Guide

2026-08-22 · 9 min read · By Freelance LeadsHub

Competing for one-off projects on Upwork and Fiverr is exhausting. You're competing with thousands of other freelancers, platforms are taking 20–30% commission, and you start from zero with every new client. The real money—and the real peace of mind—comes from repeat clients.

A repeat client who pays you ₦150,000–₦300,000 per month for ongoing work is worth infinitely more than five clients each paying ₦50,000 once and then disappearing. Not only do you get predictable income (crucial when NEPA cuts your power and you need to buy a generator), but you also build relationships that can lead to referrals, higher rates, and eventually a stable freelance career.

Here's how to systematically get repeat clients as a freelancer in Nigeria—and how to keep them coming back.

Why Repeat Clients Matter More Than You Think

Let me be direct: your first goal as a Nigerian freelancer should not be to land a thousand clients. It should be to land 5–10 clients who hire you consistently.

Here's the math:

  • Platform freelancer: 10 projects/month at ₦50,000 each = ₦500,000/month gross. Minus 20% Upwork fee = ₦400,000 net. Unstable. New pitch every week. Takes 15–20 hours of admin per month.
  • Repeat client freelancer: 5 repeat clients × ₦100,000/month average = ₦500,000/month gross. Direct payment (Payoneer, Wise, Grey) = 100% yours. Stable. Minimal new pitching. Takes 2–3 hours of admin per month.

Same income. Half the stress. Double the time for actually doing the work or landing even more clients.

Repeat clients also trust you. They don't question your rates. They don't ask for endless revisions. They refer you to other businesses. They become your safety net when one client's budget gets cut—because you're not dependent on them alone.

Step 1: Build a Repeatable Service Model (Not One-Off Projects)

The first mistake Nigerian freelancers make is offering to do "whatever the client needs." This creates one-off projects, not repeat work.

If you're a designer and you take projects like "logo design," "Instagram template redesign," "business card mockup," you're constantly starting from scratch. The client finishes, and the relationship ends.

Instead, package your service as an ongoing retainer. Examples:

  • Social media content creator: Instead of "design 5 Instagram posts," offer "4 posts per week + 2 stories + captions for ₦150,000/month."
  • Content writer: Instead of "write one blog post," offer "2 blog posts + 4 LinkedIn posts per month for ₦120,000."
  • Virtual assistant: Instead of "admin tasks as needed," offer "10 hours per week of email, scheduling, and admin tasks for ₦100,000."
  • Video editor: Instead of "edit one YouTube video," offer "2 videos per month + thumbnail design for ₦200,000."

The key is: the client knows exactly what they're paying for and when. You know exactly how much time you're spending. There's no surprise scope creep. And at the end of the month, if the client is happy, they just renew with you automatically.

Key Insight: Retainer models create repeat business. One-off projects create one-time clients. The fastest way to build repeatable income is to stop selling by the project and start selling by the month.

Step 2: Find the Right Clients Before You Pitch

Now you have your service packaged. The next step is finding businesses that actually need ongoing help—not random people on marketplace platforms.

Think about who needs monthly, ongoing work:

  • E-commerce businesses (need weekly product photos, constant content updates)
  • Service-based businesses (salons, law firms, dental clinics—need booking management, social media)
  • Manufacturing/import-export companies (need pricing lists updated, supplier communications)
  • Real estate agents (need listings updated, content creation)
  • Marketing agencies (need designers, writers, video editors on retainer)
  • SaaS/tech companies (need blog content, email sequences, social media)

These businesses have budgets. They expect to pay professional rates. And they need consistent work—the definition of a repeat client.

Step 3: Cold Email Your Way to Repeat Clients

Once you have your lead list, you need to contact them. This is where most freelancers fail—they send generic, weak emails that go straight to spam or get ignored.

Here's a cold email framework that works for repeat client acquisition:

  1. Subject line: Mention something specific about their business. "Question about your [product/service]" or "[Their business name] + [their niche]"
  2. Opening: Don't pitch. Ask a question or reference their business. "I noticed you've been selling [product] on Instagram and getting good engagement—I work with e-commerce businesses on content."
  3. The offer: Be specific. Not "I can help grow your business." But: "I create product content and manage Instagram posts. Most of my clients spend 2–4 hours per month on social and want that time back. Would it make sense to chat about how this works?"
  4. Proof: One link to your portfolio or a case study (if you have past clients). If you don't, skip this or mention past work generally.
  5. Close: A simple yes/no question. "Does this sound like something worth 15 minutes on a call?"

Example email:

Hi [Name],

I noticed you sell skincare products on Instagram and your engagement looks solid—lots of saves and comments on your posts.

I work with e-commerce brands in Nigeria on content creation and social management. Most of my clients were spending 4–5 hours per week trying to manage their own Instagram and wanted that time back. I now manage 4 accounts similar to yours, creating posts, captions, and handling DMs.

I charge ₦150,000/month for this. Does it sound like something worth a 15-minute call to see if it's a fit?

Cheers,
[Your name]
[Your portfolio or WhatsApp link]

Step 4: Price for Retainers (Not Hourly or Per-Project)

Most Nigerian freelancers on Upwork charge $10–$30/hour or ₦5,000–₦15,000 per project. This is a poverty wage. It also makes it harder to get repeat clients because you're already underpriced.

Retainer pricing is different. You're not selling hours; you're selling reliability and results.

Here's realistic retainer pricing in Nigeria (2026):

  • Content creation (4 posts/week + captions): ₦100,000–₦200,000/month
  • Virtual assistant (10 hours/week): ₦80,000–₦150,000/month
  • Social media management (2 platforms): ₦150,000–₦300,000/month
  • Video editing (2 videos/month): ₦150,000–₦250,000/month
  • Copywriting (8 emails + ad copy/month): ₦120,000–₦200,000/month

Why these numbers? Because businesses paying ₦150,000/month are making ₦1,000,000+/month in revenue. To them, paying you is a no-brainer ROI.

Step 5: Deliver Exceptional Quality (The Obvious Part)

Once you land a repeat client, the hard part is actually earning the repeat business.

This means:

  • Be on time, every time. If you said 2 posts on Monday, they're there Monday morning. Not Tuesday. Not "by end of week." Monday.
  • Communicate proactively. Don't wait for them to ask. Send weekly updates. "Here's what I did this week, here's what's coming next week, here's one thing I'm noticing." This takes 10 minutes and makes you invaluable.
  • Solve problems, don't just complete tasks. If your job is social media and you notice comments aren't being responded to, mention it. Suggest a system. Go beyond the contract.
  • Track and report results. If you manage their social media, show them engagement trends. If you write emails, show open rates. Make it obvious you're earning your fee.
  • Don't disappear. Most freelancers are responsive for the first month, then ghosting begins. Be the opposite. Be the person they can always rely on.

Reality Check: You will lose clients. Sometimes it's not your fault—their budget gets cut, they hire someone in-house, their business direction changes. This is normal. The goal is to keep 80–90% of your clients year-over-year. If you're keeping 50%, you're not delivering enough value.

Step 6: Build a Waitlist and Referral System

Once you have 5–7 repeat clients paying you ₦100,000–₦200,000/month each, you'll hit capacity. You can't take on new clients without dropping quality.

At this point, do two things:

1. Create a waitlist. Tell new prospects: "I'm fully booked, but I have a waitlist. If a spot opens up in the next 3 months, I'll let you know. My rate is ₦[amount]/month." You'll get 3–5 people on the list. When a client churns, you have an immediate replacement.

2. Ask your clients for referrals. Simple: "I'm not taking new clients right now, but if you know another business that needs help with [your service], I'd love a warm intro. I always take care of my clients' referrals." Most clients will refer you—they love you, and they want to help.

Referrals from existing clients close 3–5x faster than cold email and have 2–3x higher retention. Build this system early.

Step 7: Maintain Relationships During Slow Months

In Nigeria, freelance income is seasonal. December is slow (everyone's traveling). August can be slow. Some industries are quieter in certain months.

Here's what happens: a client pauses work for 2–3 months, and you assume the relationship is dead. Then they come back and hire someone else because they've moved on.

Instead, maintain the relationship during slow periods:

  • Send a monthly check-in email: "How's business? Anything I can help with?"
  • Share relevant articles or ideas: "Saw this trend, thought it might apply to what you're doing."
  • Offer light work at a discount: "I have availability next month—would you want me to do a content audit or competitor analysis for ₦50,000?"

This keeps you top-of-mind. When their budget returns, you're the first person they call.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How long does it take to get your first repeat client?

A: If you start cold emailing today with a solid offer and package, expect 4–8 weeks to land your first repeat client. Most people give up after 2 weeks because they send 20 emails, get no response, and think cold email doesn't work in Nigeria. It does—you just need volume. Send 200–300 emails before you judge results. Also, your first repeat clients will likely come from people you already know (past Upwork clients, mutual contacts, LinkedIn), so start there first. It's faster and higher conversion.

Q: What if a client wants to pay me less than my quoted retainer?

A: Negotiate scope, not price. Don't say "okay, I'll do it for ₦100,000 instead of ₦150,000." Say "I can deliver that at ₦150,000. At ₦100,000, I'd do 2 posts per week instead of 4. Which would work better for your budget?" Most clients will choose the lower-scope option or find the budget for the full service. If they're genuinely unable to pay your rate, they're not the right client. Move on.

Q: How do I keep repeat clients from asking for "just one more thing" without paying extra?

A: Set clear boundaries from day one. Your contract should specify exactly what's included. If they ask for something outside scope, your response is: "That's outside the scope of our agreement. I can add it for an additional ₦[X] or include it in next month's deliverables if you'd like to expand the retainer." Be friendly, but firm. Clients respect this—it shows you know your value.

Q: Should I use Upwork/Fiverr to find repeat clients, or just cold email?

A: Start with cold email. Platforms take 20–30% commission, which kills your retainer margins. However, if you already have Upwork/Fiverr clients, actively try to move them off-platform by proposing a retainer with direct payment (Wise, Payoneer, Grey). Say: "I'd like to offer you a better rate if we move to direct payment." They almost always say yes because it saves them platform fees too. Then you're building repeat clients at higher margins.

Final Word: Start With One, Build to Many

You don't need to land 10 repeat clients tomorrow. You need to land one. Then make them so happy they become a case study. Then use that success to land your second repeat client.

The path is:

  1. Package your service as a retainer (not one-off projects)
  2. Find the right businesses using a targeted lead list from Freelance LeadsHub
  3. Cold email them consistently (500+ per month) using Zaram Web Mailer
  4. Price confidently (₦100,000–₦300,000/month, not ₦5,000 per project)
  5. Deliver exceptional quality and stay in regular communication
  6. Build a waitlist and referral system
  7. Maintain relationships during slow periods

In 6–12 months, if you execute this, you'll have 5–10 repeat clients paying you ₦500,000–₦1,500,000/month. That's ₦6,000,000–₦18,000,000/year. At current exchange rates (roughly ₦1,500 = $1 USD), that's $4,000–$12,000/month USD—more than enough to live comfortably in Nigeria, pay your NEPA bill, and have time left for new projects or scaling.

The platforms won't get you there. Your network will. Your cold email discipline will. Your ability to price confidently will.
