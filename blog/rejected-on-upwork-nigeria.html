Kept Getting Rejected on Upwork Nigeria? Here's What to Do Instead

8 August 2026 · 9 min read · By Freelance LeadsHub

You've applied to 47 projects. Maybe 48. Your profile looks solid—decent portfolio, reasonable rates, clean profile picture. But the rejection emails keep coming. Or worse: you're not even getting the courtesy of a "no thanks." Your proposals are disappearing into the void.

If you're a Nigerian freelancer asking "why do I keep getting rejected on Upwork?" you're not alone. And more importantly: this rejection is actually a sign you should leave the platform entirely.

This article walks you through why rejection happens, what the real earning ceiling is on Upwork for Nigerian freelancers, and most importantly—the proven alternative strategy that lets you earn 3–5x more by going directly to business owners who actually have budget and urgency.

Why You're Getting Rejected on Upwork Nigeria (The Honest Version)

Upwork rejection isn't random. It's systematic. Here's what's actually happening:

1. Oversupply Problem: Nigeria Has 80,000+ Freelancers on Upwork

There are estimates suggesting Nigeria has anywhere from 60,000 to 100,000+ active freelancers on Upwork. For a single $500 writing project, you're competing with 200–400 proposals. The client isn't reading all of them—they're skimming the first 20 and picking someone with a 5-star review history or lower rates.

You're not being rejected because you're bad. You're being rejected because the signal-to-noise ratio is so high that good work is invisible.

2. New Account Penalty (First 3–6 Months)

Upwork's algorithm penalizes new freelancers. You start with zero JSS (Job Success Score). Until you land 2–3 paid projects and hit a 90%+ score, Upwork literally buries your profile in search results. A client searching for "Nigerian writer" will see established freelancers first—sometimes 10–15 pages of them—before they ever see your name.

Realistically: expect to spend your first 3–6 months on Upwork getting very few invitations and winning maybe 1 in every 40–50 proposals.

3. You're Competing on Price (And Losing)

If you're offering $15/hour or $8 per article, there are freelancers in Pakistan, Bangladesh, and the Philippines offering $5/hour with 4.9 stars and 500+ completed projects.

You cannot win a pure price war on Upwork. Your only move is to specialize and charge more—but that requires an established reputation first. It's a catch-22.

4. Your Proposal Template is Generic

Most rejected proposals look like this: "Hi, I'm a professional writer with 5 years experience. I'm interested in your project and can deliver quality work on time. Looking forward to hearing from you."

The client gets 150 of these. Yours is invisible. They pick the one with the personal touch: "Hi [Name]—I notice you're building a SaaS product for African markets. I've written 12 case studies for tech startups in Lagos and understand your audience intimately."

But on Upwork's saturated marketplace, even a good proposal doesn't convert often enough.

The Real Income Ceiling on Upwork for Nigerian Freelancers

Let's talk honestly about what you can actually make on Upwork from Nigeria, and when it stops being worth your time.

Realistic Monthly Income Timeline

Months 0–3: ₦0–15,000/month. You're grinding. Most new freelancers make nothing or a few hundred naira in this phase.

Months 3–8: ₦30,000–80,000/month. You've landed 3–4 projects, your score is improving, you're getting 1 or 2 proposals accepted per month.

Months 8–18: ₦100,000–250,000/month. You have a track record. Clients are inviting you directly. You're hitting $50–100/month in Upwork fees (20% cut), which means you're clearing roughly ₦150,000–400,000 before Wise/Payoneer withdrawal fees.

Year 2+: ₦250,000–600,000/month. The ceiling. At this point, you're a top-rated freelancer, you're picky about projects, and Upwork is your primary income. Roughly $500–1,200/month gross.

The Problem: ₦600,000/month ($400 USD) takes 18–24 months to reach, requires obsessive profile optimization, and you have zero control—Upwork can suspend your account, change their algorithm, or cut your JSS without warning. More Nigerians are stuck at ₦100,000/month ($65) than break through to ₦300,000/month ($200).

Why the Ceiling Exists

Upwork's platform dynamics cap your income:

  • You compete on the platform's terms: Clients see you only if Upwork's algorithm shows you. You have no direct relationship with them.
  • 20% commission eats your margins: Every dollar you earn, Upwork takes 20 cents. That's 20% compound loss on top of exchange rate fluctuations.
  • Oversupply keeps rates down: Even as a top-rated freelancer, you're still competing with thousands of others. You can't raise rates above market without losing clients.
  • Limited leverage: You have no negotiation power. If a client refuses to pay or tries to scam you, Upwork's dispute process favors the client.

The math: if you make $1,000/month on Upwork, Upwork takes $200, you pay ₦400–500 to withdraw via Wise (2.5% + 1 GBP), and you're left with roughly ₦370,000–400,000 in your Nigerian bank account. It's decent, but it took you 18+ months to reach it, and you have zero leverage.

The Strategy Winning Nigerian Freelancers Use Instead: Direct Outreach

The fastest way to replace or exceed Upwork income is to stop competing for projects on Upwork. Stop submitting proposals to strangers. Instead, find businesses that need your service and pitch them directly.

Here's how:

Step 1: Identify Your Target Business Type

Narrow down who actually pays well for your service. Examples:

  • If you're a writer: B2B SaaS companies, marketing agencies, e-commerce brands, fintech startups. (Not bloggers or content mills.)
  • If you're a designer: E-commerce brands, real estate agencies, financial services, education tech. (Not budget design contests.)
  • If you're a developer: Agencies, startups funded by VCs, enterprise software companies. (Not small local websites.)

The rule: find businesses that have revenue. Revenue = budget for outsourcing.

Step 3: Write a Cold Email Script (Not a Proposal)

Your email isn't a proposal. It's a conversation starter. It's short, specific, and it mentions a problem you noticed.

Common Objections (And Answers)

"Isn't Cold Email Spam?"

No. Cold email is personalized outreach to a relevant audience. Spam is mass, impersonal, irrelevant emails. If you're emailing a SaaS founder about a problem you solved for similar founders, you're cold email. If you're emailing someone a forex trading link, you're spam.

Use a proper tool like Zaram Web Mailer, keep your domain reputation clean, and follow best practices (personalisation, relevant subject lines, clear unsubscribe)—and you'll have a 20–30% open rate.

"What If I Get No Replies?"

You probably will get some. Industry standard is 2–4% reply rate for cold email. That means out of 500 emails, 10–20 people reply. Out of 1,000 emails, 20–40 people reply.

If you get zero replies:

  • Your subject line might be weak. Test 3–5 different subject lines on batches of 100.
  • Your email might be too long or too salesy. Keep it short and conversational.
  • You might be targeting the wrong audience. Are they really the decision-makers?
  • Your domain reputation might be tanked. Use a fresh domain or a tool that manages sender reputation.

Read our guide on improving cold email reply rates for Nigerian freelancers for more debugging.

"I Already Have Upwork Clients. Should I Quit?"

No. Keep them if they're profitable and reliable. But start direct outreach in parallel. Use 30% of your time on Upwork (keep the income), use 70% on direct outreach (build the better income stream).

Within 6 months, direct outreach will likely generate more revenue than Upwork. Then you can choose to quit Upwork or keep it as a secondary platform.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How long does it really take to replace Upwork income with direct outreach?

A: Most Nigerian freelancers see their first client within 2–4 weeks of starting cold email campaigns. However, the timeline to replace a full Upwork income (say ₦200,000/month) depends on your niche and rates. If you charge $50/hour and need 4 clients at 10 hours each per month, that's 4–6 weeks of outreach. If you charge $20/hour, it might take 8–10 weeks. The key variable is your rate—a higher-paying niche (B2B tech, finance, legal) closes faster than a lower-paying one (general writing, graphics).

Q: Do I need a website to do cold email outreach?

A: No, but it helps. A simple portfolio website (even 1 page with 3 case studies and your rates) increases trust significantly. When someone replies to your cold email interested, they'll Google you or ask to see your work. Without a website, you're telling them to check Upwork—which undermines the whole point. Spend 3–4 days building a basic portfolio on WordPress or a tool like Webflow, then reference it in your emails. It's worth 10–15% higher conversion rate.

Q: What if a client wants to pay through Upwork even after I've pitched them directly?

A: Be polite, but decline. Explain that you've moved to direct invoicing to reduce fees and pass savings to them. Offer two options: Payoneer invoice link or Wise bank transfer. Most established businesses actually prefer this—it's cleaner for their accounting. If they insist on Upwork, you can use it, but you've lost the fee advantage. Usually this happens with smaller businesses; larger companies are fine with Payoneer/Wise.

Q: Is it better to target local Nigerian businesses or international clients?

A: International clients (UK, US, Australia) typically have 3–5x larger budgets and pay in USD/GBP, which is worth more in naira. Local Nigerian businesses are easier to reach and faster to close, but they negotiate harder on price and have tighter budgets. Best move: target international clients, but pitch local businesses as a secondary strategy.
