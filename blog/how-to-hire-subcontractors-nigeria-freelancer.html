How to Hire Subcontractors as a Freelancer in Nigeria: Complete Step-by-Step Guide

08 August 2026 · 9 min read · By Freelance LeadsHub

You've hit a ceiling. Your freelance business in Nigeria is bringing in consistent work—maybe ₦500,000 to ₦2,000,000 per month—but you're working 60-hour weeks and turning down clients because you don't have capacity. Sound familiar?

The solution most successful Nigerian freelancers don't talk about: hire subcontractors. Not employees. Not full teams. Just vetted freelancers who handle overflow work while you focus on client relationships, sales, and higher-ticket projects.

This guide walks you through how to hire subcontractors as a freelancer in Nigeria—from finding the right talent to structuring payment terms that work across borders, managing deadlines, and keeping quality high. By the end, you'll know exactly how to scale without burning out.

Why Subcontractors Are Better Than Employees for Nigerian Freelancers

Before we get into the mechanics, let's be clear on why subcontractors make sense for freelancers in Nigeria specifically.

Hiring a full employee means:

  • Fixed monthly salary (typically ₦150,000–₦500,000+ depending on skill level)
  • Employer responsibilities you didn't sign up for
  • PAYE deductions, tax compliance headaches
  • Severance obligations if things don't work out
  • Losing the agility that made you a freelancer in the first place

Subcontractors, by contrast:

  • You pay only for work completed (per project or per task)
  • No ongoing salary obligation in slow months
  • They're responsible for their own taxes
  • You can scale up or down based on actual client demand
  • You retain the freedom that drew you to freelancing

If you're making ₦500,000–₦1,500,000 monthly from clients, hiring a subcontractor typically costs ₦80,000–₦250,000 per project (or per week, depending on arrangement), which you pass on to your client or absorb from the margin. The math works.

Step 1: Define What You Need (Skills, Timeline, Budget)

Don't just post "Looking for subcontractors" and see who bites. That's how you waste two weeks interviewing wrong people.

Write down specifically:

  1. The exact tasks. Are you outsourcing graphic design? Video editing? WordPress development? Cold email outreach? Content writing? Be granular. Don't say "marketing"—say "LinkedIn profile optimization for B2B SaaS clients."
  2. Your quality bar. Show examples of work you'd accept. If you're outsourcing design, share 3 portfolio pieces that match your standard. If it's coding, show a GitHub repo or demo site you approve of.
  3. Timeline and volume. "I need 5 social media graphics per week" or "I need someone to handle code reviews on 2–3 projects per month." This tells subcontractors if they have capacity.
  4. Your budget per unit. In Nigeria, rates vary wildly depending on skill:
    • Junior content writer: ₦5,000–₦15,000 per 1,000 words
    • Mid-level UI/UX designer: ₦50,000–₦150,000 per design
    • Experienced Django developer: ₦1,500–₦4,000 per hour or ₦200,000–₦500,000 per project
    • Video editor: ₦80,000–₦250,000 per minute of finished video
    Know your budget before you post.
  5. Communication and timezone needs. Do they need to be awake during your working hours? Can they work async? For Nigerian freelancers, timezone overlap with clients matters—if you're serving US clients, a West African subcontractor is ideal.

Pro tip: The best subcontractors are usually other established freelancers like you—not broke freshers looking for their first gig. They deliver faster, ask fewer clarifying questions, and understand deadlines. Target people with 3+ years of freelance experience.

Step 2: Where to Find Subcontractors in Nigeria

You have three main channels:

Option A: Freelance Platforms (Upwork, Fiverr, Toptal)

The fastest route. Post your job, filter by location (Nigeria), review portfolios, hire.

Pros:

  • Built-in escrow—you don't pay until work is done
  • Reviews and ratings visible
  • Dispute resolution if things go wrong

Cons:

  • Platform takes 5–20% commission
  • You're competing with global talent
  • Good freelancers are expensive on these platforms

Reality: On Upwork, a ₦100,000 project costs you ₦110,000–₦120,000 after fees. Still, it's worth it for risk reduction early on.

Option B: LinkedIn and Twitter/X

Post in Nigerian freelancer communities (there are hundreds of active spaces). Reach out directly to people whose work you've seen.

Pros:

  • Direct communication—no middleman
  • You can assess their professionalism in real-time
  • Often cheaper than platform rates
  • You build a personal network for future work

Cons:

  • No built-in escrow—requires trust
  • Takes longer to vet properly
  • You handle disputes yourself

Step 3: Vet and Interview Candidates

Never hire based on a nice CV alone. This is where most freelancers fail.

Here's your vetting checklist:

  1. Portfolio review (15 min). Ask for 3–5 samples. Do they match your quality bar? If you're hiring a copywriter, read the actual copy—is it tight, or does it meander? If it's a designer, do the layouts feel intentional?
  2. One test project (1–3 days). Don't hire someone for ₦500,000 in annual work without testing them first. Give them a small task (₦10,000–₦25,000 range). Pay them for it. See:
    • Do they deliver on time?
    • How do they handle revisions?
    • Do they ask clarifying questions or just assume?
    • What's the turnaround on edits?
    This one test project saves you ₦200,000+ in future headaches.
  3. Communication style (one call). Jump on a 15–20 min WhatsApp or Zoom call. Can they articulate their process? Do they seem professional? Will working together be tolerable for hours every week?
  4. Ask about their current workload. "How many clients are you working with right now?" and "How many hours per week can you commit to my projects?" A subcontractor with 8 active clients and no capacity is useless to you.
  5. Set expectations upfront. Be clear: "I need turnarounds of X days," "Revisions are included up to Y rounds," "You'll get paid via Wise/Payoneer on day Z after delivery." No surprises.

Step 4: Structure the Payment and Legal Framework

This is the unsexy part, but it matters.

Payment Channels (Nigeria to Subcontractor)

If paying within Nigeria:

  • Bank transfer (direct). Fastest, most trusted. Use your bank app (GTBank, Zenith, UBA, Access, etc.). ₦500,000 transfer takes 10 minutes. Subcontractor gets the money same day.
  • Payoneer or Wise withdrawal. If you're receiving in USD from foreign clients, keep some funds in Payoneer or Wise and pay subcontractors directly to their naira accounts via the platforms.

If subcontractor is outside Nigeria:

  • Wise (previously TransferWise). Best rates, low fees (usually ₦500–₦2,000 for transfers up to ₦500,000). Takes 1–2 business days.
  • Payoneer. Slower, slightly higher fees, but works if both parties have accounts.
  • Cryptocurrency. For tech-savvy freelancers—fast, but volatile. Not essential.

Real numbers: If you're paying a subcontractor ₦150,000, and they're in Ghana or Kenya, Wise costs you ₦1,500–₦2,000. Worth it.

Payment Terms That Work

Do NOT do 50% upfront + 50% on delivery. That's for new, untested vendors.

Once you've passed the test project, use this:

  • For ongoing retainers (weekly/monthly): "Payment is net 7 days after delivery." They deliver Monday, you pay by the following Monday. This gives you time to QA and is fair to them.
  • For large projects (₦500,000+): "30% upfront, 70% on delivery." Subcontractor covers operational costs, you keep leverage until it's done right.
  • For rush jobs or repeating small tasks: "Payment within 24 hours of delivery, via Wise/bank transfer." Speed builds trust fast.

Tax note: In Nigeria, if you pay a subcontractor ₦500,000+ in a year, you might need to file a tax return declaring that income (since they'll declare it as freelance income). It's not enforced hard, but stay aware. For now, focus on good relationships over perfect compliance—most Nigerian freelancers operate in the grey zone here.

Step 5: Manage the Relationship and Quality

Once you've hired your first subcontractor, your job changes. You're now a manager, not just a doer.

Set Up a Communication System

  • WhatsApp for quick updates. "How's the design coming?" "Sending revision notes at 3 PM."
  • Email or shared docs for detailed briefs. Never give a design brief over WhatsApp—use Google Docs or email. It's clear, it's a record, and it's professional.
  • Weekly check-ins (if ongoing work). A 10-min call Friday afternoon to review what's done, what's coming, and any blockers.

Create a Brief Template

Every project gets the same format:

  • Client name and context
  • The deliverable (e.g., "3 blog posts, 1,500 words each")
  • Your quality requirements (link to examples)
  • Deadline (be specific: "March 15 by 5 PM Lagos time")
  • Revision rounds included and how to submit feedback
  • Payment amount and timeline
  • Your availability for questions

This takes 10 minutes to write and saves 2 hours of back-and-forth.

Track Deliverables

Use a simple Google Sheet or Airtable with columns:

  • Project name
  • Subcontractor name
  • Start date, deadline
  • Status (in progress / submitted / revisions / paid)
  • Payment amount and date paid

This prevents the chaos of losing invoices and helps when tax time comes around.

Handle Quality Issues Early

If the first deliverable is mediocre:

Don't: Silently rewrite it and never hire them again.

Do: Send detailed feedback within 24 hours. "The layout feels cramped. Can you increase whitespace? Also, the CTA button should be red, not blue (see attached example)." Give them a chance to nail it.

If they nail the revision, great—you've got a keeper. If they don't understand feedback or push back, cut ties early. It's better to be out ₦25,000 than ₦500,000 down the road.

Scaling: From One Subcontractor to a Small Team

Once you've hired your first subcontractor successfully, hiring more follows the same playbook—but the economics change.

If you're pulling in ₦2,000,000 per month and spending ₦400,000 on subcontractors, your margin is still healthy. You're now a "freelance agency," even if it's just you + 2–3 other people.

The next step is finding more subcontractors faster. Instead of waiting for applications, proactively reach out to vetted freelancers in your network. Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Hiring too many at once. You can't manage 5 new subcontractors simultaneously while serving clients. Start with 1. Prove the model works. Scale to 2–3 after 3 months.
  • Being vague in briefs. "Make it look professional" is not a brief. Subcontractors aren't mind readers. More specificity = fewer revisions = faster delivery.
  • Underpaying to save margin. A subcontractor earning ₦40,000 to build a website will cut corners and cost you a client. Pay fairly (₦150,000+), or don't hire.
  • No written agreement. Even with trust, use a simple contract (one page). It should cover: deliverables, deadline, payment, revisions, and IP ownership. A lawyer can draft one for ₦15,000–₦25,000. It's worth it.
  • Ignoring timezone issues. If your subcontractor is in a different timezone and doesn't overlap your work hours, communication slows to a crawl. Prefer people in West Africa (Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya) if you're serving global clients.
  • Not testing before committing. That small test project isn't a nicety—it's essential. Do it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Should I hire subcontractors from Nigeria or abroad?

A: Hire from Nigeria first. It's easier to communicate in real-time, timezones align, and payment is simpler (direct bank transfer). Once you've proven the model with one or two Nigerian subcontractors, explore freelancers in Ghana, Kenya, or the UK if you need specialized skills. Nigerian freelancers are underrated—many have 5+ years of experience and charge 30% less than equivalent talent on Upwork.

Q: What if a subcontractor misses a deadline?

A: Build in a buffer. If the client deadline is Friday, ask the subcontractor for Wednesday delivery. That gives you 48 hours to review, request revisions, and deliver polished work on time. If they miss the Wednesday deadline, escalate: either they deliver Thursday morning with a discount, or you find someone else. Don't pay full price for late work—incentives matter.

Q: How do I protect myself legally if something goes wrong?

A: Use a simple one-page subcontractor agreement covering: deliverables, deadline, payment terms, revision limits, and IP ownership (who owns the work—usually your client). You can have a lawyer draft one for ₦15,000–₦25,000. For smaller projects (under ₦50,000), a clear WhatsApp message outlining the terms works too. The point is: no surprises. Write it down.

Q: Can I hire subcontractors while still using Upwork as my main platform?

A: Yes, but carefully. Most platforms' terms say you can't hire Upwork contractors outside the platform. However, hiring non-Upwork freelancers (via LinkedIn, direct outreach, or Freelance LeadsHub) is perfectly fine. Just don't take Upwork clients and farm them to a subcontractor—that violates terms and risks account suspension. Instead, hire subcontractors, deliver the final work yourself, and keep client relationships direct.

Bottom Line: Start Small, Prove It, Then Scale

Hiring subcontractors as a Nigerian freelancer isn't complicated, but it requires discipline:

  1. Define exactly what you need. Skills, timeline, budget, quality bar.
  2. Find candidates via platforms, personal networks, or direct outreach (using targeted lead lists like Freelance LeadsHub cuts search time by 80%).
  3. Always run a test project first. ₦10,000–₦25,000 now saves ₦500,000 later.
  4. Pay via bank transfer or Wise. Be transparent on timing and amount.
  5. Manage ruthlessly with clear briefs and weekly check-ins. Poor communication causes 90% of subcontractor problems.
  6. Scale one subcontractor at a time. Don't hire three people simultaneously.
