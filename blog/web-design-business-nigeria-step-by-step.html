How to Start a Web Design Business in Nigeria Step by Step

2026-07-26 · 9 min read · By Freelance LeadsHub

You want to start a web design business in Nigeria. Good. The barrier to entry is low—you don't need capital, you don't need an office, and you can start from your room with just a laptop and internet connection. But low barrier doesn't mean easy. You'll compete with thousands of other Nigerian designers on Upwork and Fiverr, race to the bottom on price, and struggle to find consistent clients.

This guide shows you how to build a sustainable web design business in Nigeria by skipping the freelance platforms, finding real businesses directly, and charging what you're actually worth. You'll learn the exact steps, realistic income figures, and the tools that actually work.

Step 1: Decide Your Niche and Service Offering

Most new designers try to serve "everyone." That's a mistake. You'll compete on price, get tire-kickers, and spend time on bad-fit projects.

Instead, pick a niche first. This could be:

  • E-commerce sites for online sellers (Jumia vendors, Konga sellers, Instagram businesses)
  • Service business websites (salons, gyms, consulting firms, real estate agents)
  • Agency/corporate sites for small to medium enterprises
  • Portfolio/landing pages for coaches, consultants, and professionals
  • Local business websites (Lagos-based restaurants, shops, clinics)

Each niche has different needs, budgets, and pain points. When you specialize, you become the go-to person for that industry—and you can charge accordingly.

Realistic pricing by niche (in Nigeria):

  • Basic 5-page WordPress site: ₦80,000–₦150,000 ($50–$95 USD at current rates)
  • E-commerce site (WooCommerce/Shopify): ₦200,000–₦400,000 ($125–$250 USD)
  • Custom coded site (agency-grade): ₦300,000–₦800,000+ ($190–$500+ USD)

Why these figures? Because most Nigerian small businesses have budgets in this range. Charge less and you're commoditized. Charge significantly more and they'll find a cheaper alternative or build it themselves.

Step 2: Build Your Skills and Portfolio (3–6 Months)

You don't need to be a world-class designer to win clients. You need to be good enough to solve their specific problem, plus better than the next person they'll interview.

Core skills to develop:

  1. WordPress + Elementor or Divi — This is your bread and butter. Most Nigerian businesses use WordPress because it's affordable and easy to update. Learn to build sites quickly without coding.
  2. Basic Figma or Adobe XD — Design mockups before you build. This separates you from amateurs and lets you present options to clients.
  3. Responsive design — Your sites must look good on phones. 60% of Nigerian web traffic is mobile.
  4. Basic SEO knowledge — Clients care about Google rankings. Learn to set up titles, meta descriptions, and page speed optimization.
  5. Landing page conversion basics — Understand CTAs, forms, and why button color matters. This makes your sites actually make money for clients.

How to build your portfolio:

Don't wait until you're "perfect" to get clients. Create 3–5 portfolio pieces now using:

  • Redesign real websites — Pick 3 existing Nigerian business websites and redesign them (don't publish, just show the before/after in your portfolio).
  • Build for friends/family — Offer free or discounted sites to real businesses you know (they're your first testimonials).
  • Create case studies — Document the process. Show the problem they had, the solution you built, and ideally, results (e.g., "helped increase inquiries by 40%").

Resources to learn fast (free and paid):

  • YouTube: Elementor tutorials, WordPress basics, web design principles
  • Coursera/Udemy: Web design courses (₦2,000–₦15,000 per course during sales)
  • LinkedIn Learning: Professional design + business skills (paid, but affordable annual membership)

Step 3: Set Up Your Business Foundation

You don't need to register a company to start. But you need a professional front.

Basic setup (₦30,000–₦80,000 total):

  1. Domain name (e.g., yourname.design or yourname.ng) — ₦3,000–₦10,000/year from Namecheap or local providers
  2. Simple portfolio website — Use Webflow, Framer, or a WordPress template (free–₦20,000)
  3. Email address — Use your domain (e.g., hello@yourname.design) via Gmail or Mailchimp
  4. WhatsApp Business profile — Free. Use for client communication
  5. Bank account — Use your existing Nigerian bank (GTBank, Access, FirstBank, etc.) to receive payments

You don't need a registered business yet. But once you hit ₦600,000+ in annual revenue, register as a sole proprietor with CAC—it takes 15 minutes online and costs about ₦5,000.

Step 4: Find Your First 10 Clients

This is where most designers get stuck. They build skills but don't know how to find clients. Here's the truth: The clients exist. You just need to reach them directly.

Method 1: Cold Email Directly to Businesses (Fastest)

Stop waiting for clients to find you on Upwork. Go find them.

  1. Get a targeted lead list. Use Freelance LeadsHub to download a list of businesses in your niche and location (e.g., "e-commerce businesses in Lagos" or "salons in Abuja"). You get their names, emails, phone numbers, and websites—exactly what you need to reach out. This costs ₦5,000–₦15,000 for a solid list and saves you weeks of manual research.
  2. Write a cold email template. Keep it short (100–150 words). Show that you've looked at their website, identify one specific problem (slow site, no mobile design, outdated look), and offer to help. See our cold email templates for proven examples.
  3. Send at scale. Once you have your lead list, load it into Zaram Web Mailer—it handles bulk sending, follow-ups, and avoids spam filters. You can send 50–100 personalized emails in one afternoon instead of copy-pasting all day.
  4. Follow up. Most don't reply to the first email. Send 3–4 follow-ups over 2 weeks. Expect a 2–5% reply rate, which means 1–5 conversations from 100 emails. That's enough to close 1–2 clients.

Cost: ₦5,000–₦20,000 for leads + email tool = ₦25,000 to potentially land a ₦150,000–₦300,000 project. That's a 10x return.

Method 2: Referrals and Network (Slow but Reliable)

  • Tell everyone you know you're building websites. Family, friends, church members, online communities.
  • Do great work for your first 2–3 clients. Ask for testimonials and referrals explicitly: "If you know anyone else who needs a website, I'd love to help. Here's my number."
  • Join Nigerian online business communities (Facebook groups, WhatsApp groups, LinkedIn) and participate genuinely. Help without selling. Trust builds slowly but converts reliably.

Method 3: Local Business Directories and Social Media

  • Find businesses on Instagram (e.g., salons, gyms, shops with poor/no websites) and message them directly.
  • Check Google Business profiles for businesses without websites and call/email them.
  • Look at Jumia, Konga, and Instagram business accounts—these sellers often need better landing pages and product sites.

Key insight: Your first 10 clients won't come from Upwork. They come from cold outreach, referrals, and direct contact. This is uncomfortable if you've never done it, but it's also your biggest competitive advantage. Most designers avoid it, which means less competition for you. One good lead list + persistent follow-up = 2–3 qualified conversations per month. That's all you need to keep busy for the next 6 months.

Step 5: Close Your First Project

The sales process (simplified):

  1. Discovery call (15–20 min). Listen. Ask about their current site, what's broken, what they want visitors to do. Take notes.
  2. Proposal. Send a simple proposal with: what you'll build, timeline (usually 2–4 weeks), price, payment terms, and revisions included. Keep it to 1 page.
  3. Payment upfront or 50/50 split. For first-time clients, ask for 50% upfront (to cover hosting, domains, tools) and 50% on delivery. This protects you from flakes.
  4. Kickoff form. Once payment clears, send a form asking for their brand colors, logo, content, photos, and what they want the site to do.

Realistic timeline for your first project:

  • Design mockup: 3–5 days
  • Client approval: 3–7 days (usually they're slow here)
  • Build in WordPress: 5–7 days
  • Revisions: 3–5 days
  • Testing and launch: 2–3 days

Total: 3–4 weeks. You'll be slower on your first few projects. That's normal. By project 10, you'll cut this down to 2 weeks.

Step 6: Set Up Payment and Money Systems

You're in Nigeria. Your clients may be local (in NGN) or international (in USD). You need systems to handle both.

For local clients (NGN payments):

  • Accept bank transfers directly to your Nigerian account (GTBank, Access, FirstBank, UBA, Zenith).
  • Use Paystack or Flutterwave to accept card payments (they charge ~1.5% + ₦100, but clients prefer it).
  • Keep transaction records for tax purposes.

For international clients (USD/GBP payments):

  • Payoneer: Free account, receive payments from clients worldwide, withdraw to Nigerian bank (₦500–₦1,000 fee per withdrawal).
  • Wise (formerly TransferWise): Better exchange rates than banks. ₦300–₦1,000 per transfer. Ideal if you receive frequent international payments.
  • Stripe: More professional but requires verification. Takes 5–10 business days to receive funds.

Currency reality: At current rates (~₦1,500–₦1,650 per USD), a ₦300,000 project = ~$180–$200 USD. That's fair value. Don't undersell in NGN and then wish you'd quoted in USD.

Earning targets (realistic monthly income):

  • Month 1–3: ₦50,000–₦150,000 (1–2 projects, slow closing)
  • Month 4–6: ₦200,000–₦400,000 (2–3 projects/month)
  • Month 7–12: ₦400,000–₦800,000+ (3–4 projects/month, repeat clients)
  • Year 2+: ₦800,000–₦2,000,000+ (4–6 projects/month, retainers, referrals)

These are realistic, not pessimistic. You're solving real problems for businesses that have budgets. You're not racing to the bottom on Fiverr.

Step 7: Tools You'll Need (Minimal Budget)

Design and Development:

  • WordPress (free) + Elementor (free or ₦20,000/year for Pro)
  • Figma (free tier is enough to start)
  • Google Fonts (free)

Hosting and Domains:

  • Bluehost or SiteGround for hosting (₦3,500–₦10,000/month) — you bill clients for this
  • Namecheap for domain names (₦3,000–₦10,000/year)

Client Management and Proposals:

  • Google Drive (free) for contracts and documents
  • Asana or Notion (free) to track projects
  • Canva (free) to create proposals

Client Acquisition (as discussed above):

  • Freelance LeadsHub for targeted business leads (₦5,000–₦15,000/list)
  • Zaram Web Mailer for cold email campaigns (₦2,000–₦5,000/month)

Total monthly cost to run this business: ₦8,000–₦20,000. Your first project pays for everything many times over.

Step 8: Scale and Build Systems

After your first 5–10 projects, you'll see patterns. Create systems to speed up:

  • Site templates: Build 3–4 WordPress templates for your niche. Customize, don't start from scratch.
  • Content templates: Have a standard structure (hero, about, services/products, testimonials, CTA, footer). This cuts design time by 30%.
  • Client onboarding: Create a one-page checklist of everything you need from them upfront. This prevents back-and-forth delays.
  • Proposal template: Write once, reuse 100 times. Just change the price and timeline.
  • Process documentation: Document your steps (discovery, design, build, launch). When you scale or hire, you have a system to follow.

As you grow, you might consider:

  • Offering retainer packages (₦30,000–₦50,000/month for ongoing updates and support)
  • Building for agencies as a white-label designer (they handle sales, you build)
  • Hiring another designer to handle overflow (you manage and deliver, they execute)

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  1. Underpricing: Your first instinct is to charge less to "win clients." Don't. Charge ₦100,000 minimum for a basic site. You can always negotiate down, but you can't negotiate up.
  2. Overscoping: A client says "add this, change that." Track revisions. Include 2–3 rounds in your contract. Anything beyond is extra (charged hourly at ₦10,000–₦15,000/hour).
  3. Skipping contracts: A verbal agreement costs you money later. Use a simple one-page contract. Protect yourself.
  4. No follow-up on leads: You send one cold email and expect instant replies. Follow up 3–4 times over 2 weeks. Most "no's" are just "not right now."
  5. Staying on Upwork: Upwork takes 20%. You compete on price. Your time is worth more. Move to direct outreach as fast as you can.
  6. Slow internet = slow delivery: Data in Nigeria is expensive. Get a stable connection (at least ₦5,000/month for decent internet + backup mobile data). It's part of your business cost.

Your Action Plan (Next 30 Days)

  1. Week 1: Pick your niche. Create 3 portfolio pieces (even if fake/redesigns).
  2. Week 2: Set up your domain, email, and simple portfolio website.
  3. Week 3: Download a targeted lead list for your niche. Write your cold email template.
  4. Week 4: Send cold emails using Zaram Web Mailer. Aim for 50–100 emails. Follow up daily on replies. Book 2–3 discovery calls.

By the end of month 1, you'll have booked your first client or be very close.

Bottom Line

Starting a web design business in Nigeria is achievable. You don't need a degree, you don't need capital, and you don't need permission. You need three things:

  1. Basic skills: Learn WordPress and design. 3–6 months of focused learning gets you to "good enough."
  2. A system to find clients: Stop competing on Upwork. Get a lead list, send cold emails with Zaram Web Mailer, and follow up relentlessly. This system is worth thousands of naira in saved time.
  3. Persistence: Your first client takes months. Your second takes weeks. Your tenth takes days. The curve gets better.

Most people fail because they underestimate the power of direct outreach and overestimate how much skill they need. You don't need to be perfect. You need to be better than the next person they'll call, and you need to show up.

Start now. Your first portfolio piece is due tomorrow.
