Why the Upwork/Fiverr Grind Doesn't Work for WordPress Developers

Let's be honest. On Upwork, a ₦500,000 WordPress project gets 47 bids. Your profile competes on reviews, not value. On Fiverr, clients choose based on gig price, not expertise. You end up racing to the bottom.

Here's the math:

  • Upwork route: Spend 4 hours bidding daily. Land 1 project per month at ₦80,000–₦150,000. Upwork takes 20%. You get ₦64,000–₦120,000. Plus platform outages, account holds, and algorithm changes that kill your visibility.
  • Direct outreach route: Spend 2 hours per week finding real businesses that need WordPress work. Land 2–3 clients per month at ₦300,000–₦1,000,000 per project. You keep 100%. Clients are grateful because they found a real person, not a bidding war.

Direct outreach to small businesses, agencies, and e-commerce brands in Nigeria is where the actual money is. These businesses need WordPress sites, but they're not hiring on Upwork. They're running their operations, stressed about their website, and waiting for a competent developer to call.

Crafting Your WordPress Developer Pitch

Once you have leads, your message matters more than your portfolio.

The Cold Email Formula That Works

Your email should:

  1. Open with their specific problem. Not "I'm a WordPress developer" but "I noticed your website takes 8 seconds to load" or "Your contact form isn't working."
  2. Show you looked at their site. Mention a specific plugin they use wrong, a page that's broken, or a competitor doing it better.
  3. State your unique offer. Not "I build WordPress sites" but "I speed up WordPress sites by 60%, which means more leads and less bounce rate."
  4. Include a clear next step. Not "let me know if you're interested" but "I have a 15-minute optimization audit I do for free—when works for you this week?"
  5. Keep it short. 4–5 sentences max.

Example email:

Hi [Name],

I was on your site yesterday and noticed it's running on an old version of WooCommerce. That's probably why your checkout page is slow—you're losing customers at the cart stage.

I specialize in fixing exactly this for ecommerce brands in Nigeria. Last client saw a 40% drop in cart abandonment after we upgraded and optimized their setup.

Worth a 15-min call to see if there's a quick win? I'm free Tuesday or Thursday afternoon.

[Your name]

This is direct, specific, and gives them a reason to respond.

Pricing: What to Charge Nigerian Businesses for WordPress Work

This is where most Nigerian developers leave money on the table.

Typical Project Rates (2026)

  • WordPress site setup from scratch: ₦300,000–₦800,000 (or $200–$500 USD). This includes domain, hosting, theme setup, basic plugins, and 5 pages of content.
  • WooCommerce store (ecommerce): ₦500,000–₦1,500,000 ($300–$1,000). Includes product pages, payment gateway integration (Paystack, Flutterwave), inventory setup.
  • Custom plugin/functionality: ₦400,000–₦1,200,000 ($250–$800). Per plugin. Depends on complexity.
  • Site speed optimization: ₦200,000–₦500,000 ($120–$300). Image optimization, caching, CDN setup, database cleanup.
  • Monthly maintenance/support: ₦50,000–₦150,000/month ($30–$100/month). Plugin updates, backups, security monitoring, minor edits.

The key: Local Nigerian businesses (not international clients) pay in naira. ₦500,000 ≈ $330 USD at current rates. That's enough to justify 2–3 weeks of work if you stay efficient.

International clients (UK, USA, Canada) should be quoted in USD or GBP. Use Wise or Payoneer to receive payments and convert at real rates—not the overpriced bank rates. The margin is significant.

Retainer Model (The Stable Income Play)

Monthly retainers beat one-off projects. Instead of quoting ₦600,000 for a one-time build, offer ₦80,000–₦150,000/month for 10 hours of support, updates, and minor enhancements. Over a year, that's ₦960,000–₦1,800,000 from one client. It's predictable income, and they stay loyal.

Most Nigerian small business owners prefer ₦100,000/month over a ₦600,000 upfront payment because cash flow. You get recurring revenue, they get peace of mind.

Common Mistakes Nigerian WordPress Developers Make

Mistake 1: Underselling Because You're Afraid

Nigerian developers often charge ₦100,000–₦150,000 for WordPress sites because they're afraid to ask for more. International developers charge 3–5x that for the same work. You're not less skilled—you're just afraid. Charge what you're worth. If a client won't pay, there are 50 others who will.

Mistake 2: Waiting for Clients to Come to You

Building a portfolio and waiting for referrals works eventually, but it takes 2–3 years. Outreach is faster. You'll have clients in 4–6 weeks if you send 100+ cold emails per week.

Mistake 3: Not Following Up

One email gets ignored. Follow-up emails convert. Send 2–3 follow-ups. Most "no" responses come after the 2nd or 3rd touch.

Mistake 4: Not Keeping Maintenance Clients Happy

Your easiest referrals come from existing clients. A ₦100,000/month retainer client who's happy will refer you to 3–4 other businesses in their network. Spend the extra hour fixing things for them. It pays off.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Do Nigerian businesses actually have budget for WordPress developers?

A: Yes, but it's concentrated in certain sectors. Ecommerce businesses, digital agencies, real estate companies, and SaaS startups all spend ₦500,000+ on WordPress projects annually. Small service businesses (plumbers, lawyers, therapists) have smaller budgets (₦200,000–₦400,000), but they're easier to close. Target mix of both. Your lead list should reflect this—filter by industry and you'll find the ones with real money.

Q: Should I use my real name or a company name when cold emailing?

A: Use your real name. "Hi, I'm [Your Name], WordPress developer" converts better than "XYZ Solutions." Nigerian business owners buy from people, not faceless agencies. Once you're established, you can create a company brand, but start personal. Also include a professional photo in your email signature—it increases trust.

Q: What if a client says they already have a WordPress developer?

A: Ask: "Are they handling maintenance and updates regularly? Most developers disappear after the initial build." Position yourself as the reliable maintenance/optimization partner. Many businesses have a developer who built their site 3 years ago and now ignore it. You're the upgrade. If they truly have someone good, ask for a referral: "Do you know other businesses looking for WordPress work?" Referrals from their network are gold.

Q: How long before I see results with cold email?

A: First response in 3–5 days. First call in 1–2 weeks. First signed deal in 3–4 weeks if you follow up properly. Most developers give up after 10 emails. The winners send 100+. It's a numbers game with a clear ROI.
