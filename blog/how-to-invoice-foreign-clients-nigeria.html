How to Invoice Foreign Clients as a Nigerian Freelancer: Step-by-Step Guide

August 3, 2026 · 9 min read · By Freelance LeadsHub

Invoicing foreign clients as a Nigerian freelancer isn't just about sending a PDF. It's about getting paid reliably, avoiding exchange rate losses, staying compliant with Nigerian tax law, and protecting yourself from payment disputes.

If you're earning dollars, pounds, or euros—whether you're on Upwork, Fiverr, or landing high-paying clients directly through targeted outreach—your invoicing strategy directly impacts your take-home naira.

This guide covers the exact invoicing methods, tools, and best practices Nigerian freelancers need to know.

Why Your Invoicing Method Matters (More Than You Think)

Most Nigerian freelancers don't realize that their invoicing choice costs them money. Here's why it matters:

  • Exchange rate losses: Moving $500 USD through the wrong channel can cost you ₦15,000–₦25,000 in conversion fees and bad rates.
  • Payment delays: Some methods take 5–10 business days. Others take 48 hours. When you're living paycheck to paycheck, that's the difference between paying NEPA on time or going dark.
  • Client trust: A professional invoice (with proper tax ID, payment terms, late fees) signals that you're a real business, not a side hustler. Clients pay faster and respect deadlines more.
  • Tax compliance: The Nigerian Tax Authority (FIRS) is cracking down on freelancer income. A proper invoicing system keeps you compliant and protects you if audited.

The Best Payment Methods for Nigerian Freelancers (2024 Comparison)

1. Wise (Formerly TransferWise)

How it works: Foreign clients send you money via bank transfer or card. Wise converts it at the real mid-market rate and deposits it into your Nigerian bank account (or Wise multi-currency account).

Pros:

  • Lowest fees for international transfers (0.67–1.5% depending on amount).
  • Real exchange rates—no markup.
  • Fast: money lands in your account in 1–2 business days.
  • Works with 80+ countries. Most foreign clients are familiar with it.

Cons:

  • Clients must initiate the payment. You can't pull money from them directly.
  • Requires identity verification (takes 24–48 hours).

Best for: Clients who are comfortable with bank transfers or Wise themselves. Freelancers earning $1,000+ per month.

2. Payoneer

How it works: Clients can send you money via Payoneer card or bank transfer. You withdraw to your Nigerian bank account or keep funds in Payoneer.

Pros:

  • Fast payouts to Nigerian banks (24–48 hours).
  • Accepted by most freelance platforms (Upwork, Fiverr, Freelancer.com).
  • Low withdrawal fees (2% to Nigerian bank accounts).
  • Easy referral system if you want to earn extra commissions.

Cons:

  • Exchange rates are ~2–3% worse than Wise.
  • Fees add up: 2% withdrawal + 1.5% currency conversion = 3.5% total cost on every transaction.
  • Account can be frozen without clear explanation (happens to ~5% of Nigerian users yearly).

Best for: Freelancers starting out or earning under $500/month. It's familiar, easy to use, and low-risk.

3. Grey (Eversend)

How it works: Similar to Payoneer. Clients send money to your Grey account. You withdraw to your Nigerian bank account or spend via Grey card.

Pros:

  • Growing platform with better UI than Payoneer.
  • Supports direct deposits from many international platforms.
  • Low fees (~1.5% withdrawal to Nigerian banks).

Cons:

  • Still newer than Payoneer—not all clients know it.
  • Smaller merchant network for refunds/chargebacks.

Best for: Freelancers who want a middle ground between Payoneer and Wise. Good for mid-level earners ($500–$2,000/month).

How to Write a Professional Invoice for Foreign Clients

Your invoice is a legal document. It protects you and your client. Here's what must be included:

Essential Invoice Elements

  1. Your Business Details
    • Your full name or business name
    • Your address (your home address is fine to start)
    • Email and phone number
    • Nigerian Tax ID (TIN) if registered with FIRS
  2. Client Details
    • Client's full legal name
    • Their company name (if applicable)
    • Their email and billing address
  3. Invoice Number & Dates
    • Unique invoice number (INV-001, INV-002, etc.)
    • Invoice date
    • Due date (typically 15–30 days for foreign clients)
  4. Itemized Services/Products
    • Description of work
    • Quantity and rate (in USD, GBP, or EUR—not naira)
    • Total amount
  5. Payment Terms
    • Payment method (Wise, bank transfer, Payoneer)
    • Bank details or payment account
    • Late payment terms (e.g., "1.5% interest per month after due date")
  6. Tax Information (if applicable)
    • "This invoice is issued from Nigeria. No VAT applied to services to non-Nigerian entities."

Tax Compliance: What Nigerian Freelancers Need to Know

The Nigerian Tax Authority (FIRS) now tracks foreign income reported by banks. Here's what you must do:

Do You Need to Register with FIRS?

  • If you earn over ₦400,000/month (or $300+ USD/month consistently), you should register for a Tax ID (TIN).
  • Registration is free and takes 10 minutes online at taxes.ng.
  • Once registered, quote your TIN on every invoice to foreign clients.

Do You Pay VAT?

No. As a freelancer providing services to non-Nigerian clients, you don't charge or pay VAT. Write this on your invoice: "Services provided to non-Nigerian entity. VAT not applicable."

Do You Pay Income Tax?

Yes, but it's complex. Freelance income is taxable in Nigeria. However:

  • You're entitled to deductions for business expenses (internet, software, equipment).
  • File your tax return annually via the FIRS portal.
  • Most freelancers earning ₦1–₦5M/year pay 10–15% effective tax rate after deductions.

Pro tip: Keep receipts for all business expenses. Accounting software like Wave tracks this automatically.

Setting Your Invoicing & Payment Terms (Currency Choice Matters)

Should You Invoice in USD, GBP, EUR, or NGN?

Simple answer: Always invoice in the client's currency, not naira. Here's why:

  • USD is king. 80% of foreign clients are comfortable paying in USD. Use this as your default.
  • UK/EU clients: If they're from the UK or EU, ask if they prefer GBP or EUR. Most will say yes.
  • Never invoice in NGN to foreign clients. It confuses them and makes you look unprofessional. You lose control of exchange rates.

Payment Terms: How Long Should Clients Have to Pay?

  • Upfront (50%) + On Completion (50%): Best for new clients. Protects you from non-payment.
  • Net 7 or Net 14: Payment due in 7–14 days after invoice. Standard for established clients.
  • Net 30: Payment due in 30 days. Only offer this to repeat clients with strong payment history.

Add a late payment clause: "Invoices unpaid after the due date will incur 1.5% interest per month."

Common Invoicing Mistakes Nigerian Freelancers Make

  • Forgetting to include late payment terms: You lose leverage. Clients think they can pay whenever.
  • Invoicing in naira: Confuses foreign clients and makes you seem unprofessional.
  • Not getting a written agreement first: Always have a scope of work signed BEFORE you invoice. Prevents disputes.
  • Using "Freelancer" as your business name: Use your actual name. It looks more professional on invoices.
  • Not following up on unpaid invoices: Send a friendly reminder at day 7, then a firm one at day 14. Most clients forget.
  • Choosing payment methods based on familiarity, not savings: Using Payoneer when Wise would save you 1.6%? That's money left on the table.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I invoice foreign clients if I don't have a Nigerian Tax ID?

A: Yes, you can. A TIN (Tax ID) is not required to invoice—it's required to be compliant with Nigerian tax law. If you're earning under ₦400,000/month, registration isn't mandatory by law, though it's still recommended for credibility. However, once you exceed that threshold or your bank reports your income, FIRS will look for your TIN. It takes 10 minutes to register at taxes.ng, so do it early. Include your TIN on every invoice once you have one—it signals professionalism and legitimacy to clients.

Q: How do I handle currency fluctuations when invoicing?

A: Always invoice in the client's currency (usually USD), not naira. You're not responsible for exchange rate changes—that's the client's problem. The exchange rate at the time of payment is what matters. If a client pushes back saying the naira got stronger and they want to pay less, stick to your original USD amount. That said, if you're worried about naira depreciation between invoice and payment, you can add a clause like "Payment due within 10 days to lock in current rates." For large projects ($3,000+), consider asking for 50% upfront to reduce your forex exposure.

Q: Should I include my personal bank account details on invoices sent to foreign clients?

A: No. Never share your personal bank details on an invoice sent to a foreign client. Instead, use a payment gateway like Wise, Payoneer, or Grey—give them those details. This protects your account from fraud and keeps your banking private. If they ask for a direct bank transfer, you can provide your Nigerian bank details, but only after they've confirmed they're ready to pay and you've verified they're legitimate. Better yet, direct them to your Wise account, which acts as a buffer and protects your main bank.

Q: What happens if a foreign client disputes a payment after I send the invoice?

A: This is why having a signed contract BEFORE starting work is critical. If a client disputes after payment: 1. Check your original scope of work document—did you deliver what was promised? 2. Document all communication (emails, Slack messages, WhatsApp conversations). 3. If using Wise or Payoneer, file a dispute claim with them (though they rarely side with the service provider). 4. If using a bank transfer, your options are limited—which is why payment upfront or via escrow is safer. To prevent this: Always get 50% upfront for new clients, use clear project scopes, and send regular progress updates.
