How to Find Local Businesses Without Websites to Pitch in Nigeria: A Freelancer's Complete Guide

27 July 2026 · 9 min read · By Freelance LeadsHub

You're a freelancer in Nigeria. You've got real skills—maybe you do social media management, web design, copywriting, or accounting. But here's the problem: most of your potential clients don't have websites. They're running their businesses from WhatsApp, a shop on Lagos Island, an office in Abuja, or a warehouse in Kano. They're not on Upwork. They're not browsing Fiverr. They don't even know freelancers exist.

So how do you find local businesses without websites to pitch in Nigeria?

This is the exact question that separates freelancers making ₦50,000/month from those making ₦500,000+/month. The ones who figure this out stop competing with thousands of freelancers for crumbs on job boards. Instead, they own their own pipeline of clients.

Here's what you need to know.

Why Local Businesses Without Websites Are Your Goldmine

Let me be direct: most Nigerian businesses don't have websites. According to recent surveys, over 60% of small and medium businesses in Nigeria operate without an online presence. That's not a problem—that's an opportunity.

These businesses are:

  • Willing to pay. They're profitable enough to run without marketing. They're just uninformed about digital services.
  • Less competitive. Fewer freelancers are pitching them because they're harder to find.
  • Desperate for help. They know they're losing money by not being online, but they don't know how to fix it.
  • Loyal clients. Once you prove yourself, they stick around. They're not shopping around on job boards.

A fashion boutique owner in Victoria Island isn't looking for a ₦5,000 logo design. She's willing to pay ₦200,000–₦500,000 for someone to set up her Instagram, run ads, and manage her online sales. A real estate agent in Lekki needs a website—and he'll pay ₦150,000–₦300,000 for it. A restaurant owner needs Google My Business setup and WhatsApp automation—another ₦100,000–₦250,000 project.

These are the clients that turn freelancing into a real business.

Manual Methods to Find Local Businesses Without Websites

1. Google Maps & Google Search

Start simple. Open Google Maps. Search for "restaurants near me" or "real estate agents in Lekki" or "fashion boutiques in Yaba." You'll find hundreds of businesses. Now here's the key: check their Google Business Profile.

If they have a Google Business listing but no website link, they're a perfect target. Their profile might say "No website" or just have a phone number. These are exactly who you want to pitch.

How to collect contacts:

  1. Search your target niche + location on Google Maps.
  2. Click on each business and note the phone number and email (if visible).
  3. Visit their Google Business profile—some list WhatsApp or email.
  4. Use their phone number to find them on WhatsApp (most Nigerian businesses respond faster on WhatsApp than email).

Time investment: High. You can find 20–30 leads per hour, but it's repetitive work. Data cost: Minimal—just your internet. Cost to reach out: Free if using WhatsApp; ₦0.50–₦1.50 per SMS if you prefer SMS outreach.

2. Business Directories & Classified Sites

Websites like Jiji.ng, Olx.ng, and Nairaland classifieds list thousands of Nigerian businesses. You can search by category and location, then filter for those without websites or online presence.

Navigate to the relevant category (e.g., "Services" or "Business"), search your niche, and note the contact details. Many business owners on these platforms post because they're not tech-savvy—perfect targets for your pitch.

Other directories:

  • Jumia Services (services.jumia.com.ng)
  • Konga Shop (for retail businesses)
  • Local Facebook Business Pages in your target area

Time: Medium. 15–20 leads per hour. Cost: Free.

3. WhatsApp Business Directories & Groups

Nigerian business owners hang out in WhatsApp groups. Join WhatsApp communities focused on your niche—"Lagos Business Owners," "Kano Entrepreneurs," "Abuja Small Business Forum," etc. You'll find direct contacts for business owners, and they're actively discussing their challenges.

Important: Don't spam. Introduce yourself as a freelancer offering a specific service, then move conversations to 1-on-1 DMs with interested parties.

Time: 10–15 leads per hour (plus engagement time). Cost: Free.

4. Local Chambers of Commerce & Trade Associations

Organizations like the Lagos Chamber of Commerce & Industry, Kano Chamber of Commerce, and sector-specific bodies (e.g., Real Estate Developer's Association, Restaurant Association) maintain member lists. Some are public or semi-public.

Contact these organizations and ask for business member directories. Many will share them (sometimes for a small fee of ₦2,000–₦10,000). This gives you vetted, organized leads with contact details.

Time: 1–2 hours to find the right contact. Cost: ₦0–₦10,000.

5. Local Business Networks & Events

Attend local business meetups, networking events, trade shows, and industry conferences in your area. Lagos has countless entrepreneur meetups; Abuja, Kano, and Port Harcourt do too. You'll meet decision-makers face-to-face and build relationships far faster than cold email.

One event can give you 10–20 qualified leads in 3 hours.

Cost: ₦500–₦5,000 per event. Time: 3–4 hours. ROI: Often the highest because trust is built in person.

The Problem With Manual Methods (And Why You Need a Better Solution)

Real talk: Manual lead hunting works, but it's slow and exhausting. At 20 leads per hour, you'd need 50 hours of work to build a pipeline of 1,000 qualified leads. That's over a week of full-time work just to compile a list—time you could spend closing deals, not building them.

Here's what actually happens:

  1. You spend hours on Google Maps or Jiji.
  2. You write down phone numbers in a notebook or spreadsheet (your data costs ₦5–₦10/hour).
  3. You manually enter data into your CRM or email list.
  4. You realize you missed contact info (email vs. phone only).
  5. You have gaps: 200 leads, but only 80 have email addresses for outreach.
  6. You still don't have verified contact data.
  7. You start cold-calling or WhatsApp-ing with low response rates (5–10%).

And if you want to scale? You're stuck. Manual methods don't scale past a few hundred leads.

How to Actually Pitch Local Businesses (Template & Process)

Having leads means nothing if you can't pitch. Here's what works:

Step 1: Research the Business (5 minutes)

Look up the business on Google, WhatsApp, or their Facebook page. Understand what they do. A pitch to a boutique owner is different from a pitch to a real estate agent. Generic pitches have 1–2% response rates. Personalized pitches have 15–25%.

Step 2: Identify the Problem They Have

Real estate agents don't have websites = lost leads online. Fashion boutiques don't have Instagram = no online sales. Restaurants don't have Google My Business = customers can't find them easily. Nail the problem in your first sentence.

Step 3: Send a Cold Email (Not WhatsApp First)

Yes, email. WhatsApp feels more personal, but cold WhatsApp pitches often get ignored or blocked. Email is professional, archived, and trackable. Send a short (3–4 sentence) cold email first.

Template:

Hi [Name],

I noticed [Business Name] doesn't have a website yet—and you're probably losing customers because of it. Most people search Google before visiting a shop, and if you're not there, they go to competitors.

I help businesses in [Industry] build and manage websites + Google listings. Most of my clients see 30–50 new customer inquiries per month within 90 days.

Are you open to a quick chat?

[Your Name]
[Your Phone]
[Your Title/Service]

Send 50–100 of these per week. Expect 5–15 responses. Follow up with non-responders after 3 days and again after 7 days using Zaram Web Mailer—it automates this so you're not manually sending follow-ups.

Step 4: Qualify on WhatsApp

Once they respond to email, move to WhatsApp. Ask discovery questions: What's your main challenge? What's your budget? When do you want to start? This is where you separate serious prospects from tire-kickers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Is it legal to cold email businesses in Nigeria?

A: Yes. Nigeria doesn't have strict anti-spam laws like the US (CAN-SPAM) or EU (GDPR). However, best practice is to send genuinely useful emails to real business contacts—not generic spam blasts. If you're offering a real service and using professional email, you're fine. Avoid using fake sender names or misleading subject lines. Treat cold email as a professional outreach tool, not spam, and you'll have no legal issues.

Q: What niche should I target if I'm starting out?

A: Target a niche where you have expertise and where businesses are losing money without digital services. Avoid "Marketing" as a broad category—it's too competitive. Instead, pick: Social Media Management for Fashion Boutiques + Restaurants, Website Design for Real Estate Agents, Bookkeeping for E-commerce Sellers, or Google Ads Management for Law Firms. A narrow niche with deep expertise beats a broad niche with shallow knowledge. When you get traction, you can expand.

Q: How do I handle payment from local businesses in Nigeria?

A: Use bank transfers (most Nigerian businesses prefer this), or set up payment via Payoneer, Wise, or Grey. For ongoing clients (e.g., ₦150,000/month social media management), ask for payment on the 1st of each month via bank transfer. For project-based work (e.g., ₦500,000 website build), ask for 50% upfront and 50% on delivery. Most Nigerian business owners respect this structure and pay on time if you deliver results.

Q: How long before I see revenue from cold email outreach?

A: Your first responses come within 48 hours. Your first calls/meetings happen by day 3–5. Your first closes happen by week 2–3. So if you start Monday with a 10,000-lead list, you could have your first paid client by mid-week of the following week—but most people take 3–4 weeks to close their first 2–3 deals. Once you have a system (lead list → email → follow-up → call → close), you're adding clients every week.
